Reading & Writing Guide

General strategy Craft & Structure Information & Ideas Conventions Expression of Ideas

General strategy (use on every question)

  1. Read the question first, then the passage. Knowing what you're looking for makes the passage faster to read.
  2. Predict before you peek. For fill-in-the-blank and "most logically completes" questions, decide what the blank should say in your own words, then find the match.
  3. The answer is in the text. The right answer can always be proven with words in the passage. If you have to add an assumption, it's probably wrong.
  4. Watch for traps: answers that are true but don't answer the question; answers that go too far ("always," "all," "proves"); answers that use the right words in the wrong way.
  5. Pace: about 71 seconds per question. Grammar questions usually take less time, so bank that time for the harder reading ones. Mark-and-move on anything that takes over 2 minutes.

1. Craft and Structure (≈28%)

Words in Context

Pick the word that's most logical and precise in the blank, or tell what an underlined word means here.

  • Find the clue: a colon or semicolon (explains), "although/but/however" (opposite), "and/also" (same direction).
  • Predict a simple word ("careful," "hide," "short"), then match it to the fancier choice.
  • For "most nearly means" questions, plug each choice into the sentence. Common words often have uncommon meanings (check = restrain, reflect = show).
Worked example

Despite the team's ______ start, losing its first four games, it went on to win the championship.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?

  1. dismal
  2. promising
  3. memorable
  4. rapid
Show answer

Answer: A. "Despite" means the start contrasts with winning; losing four games is a bad start. Dismal means gloomy or poor.

Text Structure and Purpose

Asks why the author wrote the text or a sentence, or how the text is organized.

  • Use verbs: does the text argue, describe, explain, compare, challenge, illustrate?
  • For an underlined sentence, ask "What would be lost if I deleted this?" Look at the sentence before and after it.
  • Wrong answers often describe a detail instead of the purpose of the whole thing.

Cross-Text Connections

Two short texts on the same topic. You'll be asked how one author would respond to the other.

  • Summarize each text in one sentence before reading the choices.
  • Usually the second author partly agrees and partly disagrees, or adds a complication. Avoid extreme answers ("would reject entirely").

2. Information and Ideas (≈26%)

Central Ideas and Details

  • Main idea = what the whole passage is about, often found where the text turns ("However," "Instead," "In fact").
  • Detail questions: go back and find the exact line; don't rely on memory.

Command of Evidence: Textual

  • "Which finding would most directly support/weaken the claim?" First restate the claim in your own words, then pick the finding that connects to every part of it.
  • "Which quotation best illustrates the claim?": the quote must show the specific trait or idea, not just be about the same character or topic.
Worked example

A researcher claims that a city's new crosswalk signals reduced pedestrian accidents.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the claim?

  1. Pedestrian accidents fell 30% at intersections with the new signals but stayed the same at intersections without them.
  2. The new signals use energy-efficient bulbs.
  3. More people walk downtown on weekends.
  4. Pedestrian accidents are most common at night.
Show answer

Answer: A. The claim links the signals to fewer accidents. Only A shows accidents dropping where the signals were added and not elsewhere, which is a fair comparison.

Command of Evidence: Quantitative (tables and graphs)

  • Read the title, axis labels, and units first.
  • Find the claim in the text, then pick the choice that is both accurate to the data and supports that claim. Many wrong choices are accurate but irrelevant.
  • Check comparisons carefully: "largest increase" is about change, not about the biggest number.

Inferences

  • The blank is the logical conclusion of the passage. Ask "What must be true, given everything above?"
  • The right answer is usually modest ("likely," "may"). Eliminate anything the passage doesn't support.

3. Standard English Conventions (≈26%)

These are the fastest points on the test because the rules are limited and learnable.

Boundaries (punctuation)

To join…Use
Two complete sentences (independent clauses)period · semicolon (;) · comma + FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so)
Complete sentence → explanation or listcolon (:) or a single dash (—)
Extra (nonessential) info in the middle of a sentencea matching pair: two commas, two dashes, or two parentheses
Intro phrase/clause → main sentencecomma
Subject → its verb, or verb → its objectno punctuation
Comma splice alert: words like however, therefore, nevertheless, then are not FANBOYS. Between two sentences they need a semicolon or period before them: "It rained; however, we played."
Worked example

The ancient city was buried by volcanic ash in 79 CE ______ its ruins were not rediscovered for more than 1,600 years.

Which choice conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  1. CE,
  2. CE;
  3. CE
  4. CE, and,
Show answer

Answer: B. Both sides are complete sentences, so they need a semicolon (or a comma + FANBOYS with nothing after "and"). A is a comma splice and C is a run-on.

Form, Structure, and Sense

  • Subject-verb agreement: cross out prepositional phrases ("The box of old letters is…"). Each, every, a team, a collection = singular. With "neither…nor," match the closer subject.
  • Verb tense: match the time clues in the sentence ("last year" → past; "by the time … arrived" → had + verb).
  • Pronouns: it/its for singular things and groups; they/their for plurals. its = possessive, it's = it is. who = subject, whom = object.
  • Possessives: one student's book, two students' books, the children's books.
  • Modifiers: an opening phrase describes whatever comes right after the comma. "Walking home, I saw…" not "Walking home, the rain…"
  • Parallelism: items in a list keep the same form.

4. Expression of Ideas (≈20%)

Transitions

Cover the choices. Read the sentence before and after the blank and decide the relationship:

RelationshipTransitions
Same direction / addAdditionally, Moreover, Furthermore, Similarly, Likewise
ContrastHowever, In contrast, Nevertheless, Nonetheless, Still, Conversely
Cause → effectTherefore, Thus, As a result, Consequently, Accordingly
Example / detailFor example, For instance, Specifically
Time / sequenceFirst, Next, Then, Later, Finally, Meanwhile
RestateIn other words, That is

Rhetorical Synthesis (student notes)

  • Read the goal in the question first ("emphasize a difference," "introduce the study to a new audience").
  • Ignore the notes at first. Check each choice against the goal. Every choice is usually accurate; only one meets the goal.
  • "Difference/similarity" needs both things mentioned. "Unfamiliar audience" usually needs a definition or explanation.
